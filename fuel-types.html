91, 95, 98, E10 or diesel? Fuel types explained

Written by the Wayaroo team, Melbourne. Updated 22 November 2026.

An Australian service station can have six or more nozzles: unleaded 91, premium 95, premium 98, E10, diesel, premium diesel, sometimes LPG and a separate pump for AdBlue. The price gap between them can be 20 cents a litre or more. This page explains what each one is, what the law says about it, and how to choose without paying for something your car does not need.

What the numbers mean

The numbers 91, 95 and 98 are the petrol's Research Octane Number (RON). Octane is not energy and not "strength". It measures how well the petrol resists burning too early in the engine, which drivers hear as knocking or pinging. Engines with high compression, often turbocharged or high-performance engines, need petrol with a higher octane number. Most ordinary engines are designed for 91.

What the Australian standards say

  • Petrol: the national fuel standard sets the quality of 91 RON and 95 RON petrol. 98 RON petrol must meet the 95 RON standard.
  • Ethanol: petrol sold in Australia may contain up to 10 per cent ethanol. This is E10, and the pump must be labelled.
  • E85: a separate fuel with 70 to 85 per cent ethanol, only for vehicles made for it.
  • Sulfur: since December 2025 all grades of petrol are limited to 10 parts per million of sulfur. Because of the disruption to world oil supply in 2026, a temporary limit of 40 parts per million applies until 30 June 2027. The government says this does not affect how engines work.

The Australian Government (the Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water) sets these standards, and inspectors from the National Measurement Institute test fuel at service stations.

Which petrol does your car need?

  1. Look inside the fuel flap or in the owner's manual. It gives the minimum octane, for example "91 RON minimum" or "95 RON only".
  2. Never use less than the minimum. A car that needs 95 can be damaged by 91 over time.
  3. More than the minimum rarely helps. A car designed for 91 gets little or no benefit from 95 or 98. You pay more for the same trip.
  4. E10 is usually 91 with ethanol added. The ethanol raises the octane (a common E10 is about 94 RON), but ethanol has less energy than petrol, so a car uses a little more of it per kilometre. Compare the price per litre with that in mind.
  5. Check that your car can use E10. Most petrol cars sold in Australia in recent decades can, but some older cars, motorcycles, boats and small engines cannot. The owner's manual or the maker can tell you.

Diesel, premium diesel and biodiesel

Diesel engines need diesel, never petrol. "Premium" diesels have extra additives; your car's maker will say whether it needs one. Biodiesel blends such as B20 (20 per cent biodiesel) have their own national standard and suit only vehicles that the maker approves for them.

Wrong fuel? If you put petrol in a diesel car, or diesel in a petrol car, do not start the engine. Tell the station staff and call your roadside assistance service. Starting the engine spreads the wrong fuel through the system and can turn a tank drain into a large repair bill.

LPG (Autogas)

LPG is a gas kept as a liquid under pressure. Cars must be built or converted for it. It has its own national standard ("Autogas"). Fewer stations sell it than ten years ago, so plan your stops if you drive an LPG car.

AdBlue is not a fuel

AdBlue is the best-known brand name for diesel exhaust fluid (also called AUS 32): a solution of 32.5 per cent urea in very pure water. Many modern diesel cars and trucks have a system called selective catalytic reduction (SCR), which sprays it into the exhaust to turn harmful nitrogen oxides into nitrogen and water. It goes into its own tank, usually with a blue cap, never into the fuel tank. If the AdBlue tank runs empty, many vehicles will not restart until it is filled.
