1. Wayaroo is a guide only

Wayaroo shows information to help you plan: live vehicle positions, timetables, routes, travel times, level-crossing and crash-hotspot warnings, pedestrian and school crossings, school zones, wildlife areas, speed humps, sharp bends, unsealed roads, water crossings, cattle grids, traffic-light waiting times, traffic colours and road speeds measured from buses, trams and other drivers, level crossings where a live train is due, speed limits, cameras, road closures, fuel and trip costs, area figures, weather and more. Most of it comes from third parties and can be wrong, incomplete, out of date or missing. Estimates (for example how long boom gates will be down, how long you will wait at a traffic light, how slow the traffic is, how late a bus is, what a trip costs, or in Your day whether you walked or drove and your top speed) are guesses.

Always follow road signs, signals, boom gates, traffic police and the road rules – they override anything Wayaroo shows. A missing warning does not mean there is no hazard. A warning ahead comes earlier on faster roads (about 25 seconds of driving before it), and the time shown to it is an estimate from your current speed: it can be late, early or missing. Traffic-light waiting times are worked out from how long other drivers and buses waited there before; they do not come from the traffic-signal system and are not a signal to go. The app shows them in a red lamp as an estimate of the most that may be left (“≤40” means at most about 40 seconds more) and stops showing the number before the end. The lamp is red while your vehicle stands at traffic lights and turns green only after your own vehicle has driven off: it shows what your vehicle did, not what the traffic light shows. Move only when the light is green and the way is clear. Check important information with the official source before relying on it.

2. Driving safely

This applies in the iPhone and Android apps, on the website and on car screens (Apple CarPlay). Set up your trip before you start driving. Don't hold or touch your phone while driving unless the road rules in your state allow it (for example a phone fixed in a commercially designed mount). Wayaroo speaks warnings so you can keep your eyes on the road. Never use Wayaroo in a way that distracts you or puts anyone at risk. Learner and P-plate drivers may not be allowed to use a phone at all while driving.

3. Not for emergencies

Wayaroo is not an emergency service and is not an official warning service. Satellite heat spots are not official fire warnings. The SOS button of the Family & friends map only tells the people in your group; it does not call 000 or any emergency service, and nobody may see it in time. "Share my location while Wayaroo is closed" depends on iOS: iPhone can send the position late or not at all (for example in Low Power Mode, with no signal, or when Wayaroo's location is not set to "Always"), so a position that the group sees can be old; it uses some battery. The windscreen camera's speed sign reading is an aid and can read a sign wrong or miss it; the signs on the road always apply. Mount the phone where the law where you drive allows it, and do not touch it while driving. Crash alerts are an estimate from your phone's sensors: they can miss a crash or see one that did not happen, they work only while Wayaroo can run, and they also only tell your group. In an emergency call 000. For official warnings use your state's emergency service (for example VicEmergency).

Wayaroo is independent. It is not affiliated with or endorsed by any government, police service, transport operator or emergency service. The bodies that publish the data remain its official source: every card says where its facts come from, and how old they are.

4. Area information

Area profiles are Australian Bureau of Statistics census figures for whole areas (including median rent and mortgage repayments) and don't say anything about any person or household, or match any particular home. Don't use them as the only basis for decisions about where to live, buy or rent; get independent advice.

5. Community reports

Reports are made by users and are not checked. Only report things you have seen yourself. Don't post personal information, offensive or misleading content, or anything unlawful; such reports may be removed. Reporting in Wayaroo does not notify your council or the police. A speed hump or a pedestrian crossing that people report is shown and warned about as a report, not as an official road feature; it stays up to 14 days, and goes sooner when people say it has gone.

6. Sponsored spots

No pop-ups or banners while you drive. Wayaroo is built to show no banner, no sponsor card and no full-screen advertisement during a trip, while the phone is connected to a car's screen, or when the phone's position shows that it moves faster than 1 km/h. 7. Parking, trucks, planes and the rest

Parking rules shown for a bay come from the council's sign data and can be out of date; the sign at the kerb is what applies, and any fine is yours. The rules shown for your parked car are those of the nearest bay that has sign data, which can be a different bay from yours (the next bay, or a bay across the street): read the sign at your own bay, also when you take the time limit that Wayaroo offers. "OK to park", "Not OK to park" and the time to move the car by are worked out from that data, or from the words that were read from your photo of a sign. Wayaroo does not read arrows or symbols on a sign, a photo can be read wrongly, and a sign can change: the sign at your bay is what applies, and a parking fine stays yours. The distance and the arrow of the Find screen of a Family & friends group come from the two phones' GPS, which can be 50 metres or more out inside a building. Rules shared by other drivers come from their photos and can be wrong or out of date. The lease figures are estimates from the readings you give: your lease contract and the leasing company decide what you pay. Car health shows what the car's computer and the OBD2 adapter report, which can be wrong or missing; Wayaroo is not a mechanic. Clearing fault codes does not repair a car and resets its emission tests: do not use it to hide a fault from a buyer or an inspector. Live values and clearing show only when the car is parked. Phone charging points come from OpenStreetMap: a place can be closed, paid or gone. Fuel prices come from government price schemes and from other drivers; a price can change before you arrive, and the price at the pump is the one that applies. "Fuel left" and "Fuel is low" are estimates: Wayaroo counts from the full tank that you confirm, the tank size and fuel use that you set for your car, and the drives that Wayaroo records; a drive that Wayaroo did not record is not counted, so the estimate can be too high. Your car's own fuel gauge is what applies. Do not rely on Wayaroo to avoid running out of fuel. A taxi fare shown for a trip is an estimate from the Victorian Government's maximum fares for a taxi taken from a rank or hailed in the street: the taxi's meter is what applies, tolls, an airport fee and a card fee are extra, and booked taxis and rideshare services set their own prices. Toll prices shown for a drive are the car prices that Linkt and EastLink publish, for the toll roads the route uses; the toll operator's charge is what applies. With "Avoid toll roads" on, Wayaroo looks for a way with no toll road that it knows, and it tells you when it finds none. A road can be a toll road that Wayaroo does not know: the signs on the road are what apply, and a toll stays yours. Traffic camera pictures belong to the road authority that publishes them; where an authority gives no licence to show its pictures, Wayaroo shows only where a camera is and a link to the authority's own page. Aircraft positions and landing times come from hobbyist receivers and are estimates for curiosity only, not for aviation or anything safety-related. If you share your trip, anyone with the link can see where you are until you stop. AI answers (off until you turn them on) can be wrong, also when they are about your trip; the road signs and the road rules apply, not the answer.

Caravans, trucks and tall vehicles. If you switch on this setting, Wayaroo compares the height, length and mass that you enter with the height, weight and length limits in OpenStreetMap, which volunteers map. Many limits are not in that data and some are wrong. Wayaroo may warn you about a limit, may choose a way with no known limit, or may say that it found no way around one. A route with no warning is not a safe route: it is only a route where the map data shows no limit. Long routes are not checked before the trip. You must read every clearance sign and load sign, know the real size of your vehicle with its load, and stop if you are not sure. Wayaroo is not an approved navigation system or route planner for heavy vehicles, and it does not replace a permit, an approved route or the National Heavy Vehicle Regulator's route planner.

Notices in the app. Before your first trip, and before you switch on the caravan, truck or tall vehicle setting, the app asks you to accept a short notice. The notices say what these terms say; they do not replace them.

Move and Budget. Move works out distance, pace, calories, best efforts and fitness numbers from your phone's GPS. They are estimates, not medical or training advice. Budget works out totals, regular payments and what is left until your next pay from the amounts you type in and the bank files you bring in. They are estimates, not financial advice; your bank's own records are what apply.

Car logbook. The car logbook makes a file for the logbook method of the Australian Taxation Office (ATO) from the drives that Wayaroo recorded. Wayaroo calculates the odometer readings from the recorded km and the one reading that you type in, so they can be different from your car's odometer. Check them against the car, and add the reason and the destination of each work drive. The file helps you keep records; it is not tax advice. Wayaroo is not connected with the ATO. The ATO's rules apply, and you are responsible for what you claim.
