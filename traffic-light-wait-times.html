How long will this red light take?

Written by the Wayaroo team, Melbourne. Updated 20 November 2026.

Some navigation apps in China show a countdown at every red light. Drivers in Australia often ask why they cannot have the same. The short answer: Australian traffic lights work differently, and the road rules limit what a phone may show you while you drive. This page explains how traffic-light timing works here, what an app can honestly tell you, and how Wayaroo does it.

Most lights here change their timing all the time

Most traffic lights in Melbourne and Sydney, and in many other Australian cities, are run by a system called SCATS (Sydney Coordinated Adaptive Traffic System). Sensors in the road count the cars on each approach. The system then changes how long each green lasts, and how long the whole cycle lasts, from one cycle to the next. A green that lasted 40 seconds at 8 am can last 25 seconds at 8:05.

This is good for traffic, but it means there is no fixed timetable to read. An app cannot know the exact second a light will turn green unless it receives live data from the traffic system. Victoria publishes the places of its traffic signals and counts of vehicles in 15-minute blocks as open data, but it does not publish the live state of each light.

How the countdown apps in China work

The big map apps in China mostly do not get the signal state from the police. They work it out from their own users. Millions of phones stop at a light and start to move again. When many cars at the same light start to move at the same moment, that moment is the start of green. Repeat this over days, and the app can find how long the cycle is at each time of day. Road tests in China found these countdowns are usually 1 to 2 seconds out, and sometimes up to 5 seconds.

This works best where lights run a fixed plan. With adaptive lights like SCATS, past days are a weaker guide, and the app needs fresh starts from the last few minutes at the same light. That needs a very large number of users.

What the road rules say about your phone

In Victoria, a driver with a full licence may use a phone for navigation only when it is in a commercially made holder fixed to the car. The driver may touch it briefly to use a navigation function, but may not type, scroll, or look at messages, photos or websites. This also applies when you are stopped at a red light. Learner and P-plate drivers have stricter rules. So a useful red-light feature must need no touches at all.

How Wayaroo estimates the wait

  • From real waits. When a Wayaroo driver stops at a light and drives off, the phone sends only the light, the seconds waited, the time band (for example weekday morning peak) and the side of the junction it came from. No name, account or trip is kept. Live bus positions add more waits where few drivers use Wayaroo.
  • An upper estimate, not a promise. Half of all waits are longer than the average wait. So Wayaroo shows the most that is likely to be left: "≤38" means at most about 38 more seconds, based on the longest usual waits (the 90th percentile). The light usually changes before the number ends.
  • It stops before the end. The number disappears when less than 5 seconds may be left. Wayaroo never shows 0 and never says "go".
  • Green means your car moved. The lamp turns green only after your own car has driven off and reached the light. It shows what your car did, not the state of the signal.
  • Nothing to touch. The lamp appears by itself on the speedometer and on the map when you stop at lights.

Always obey the signal itself. Move only when the light is green and the way is clear.

Why a side street waits longer

At a junction of a main road and a side street, SCATS usually gives the main road a long green and the side street a short one. Drivers on the side street can wait two or three times as long. That is why Wayaroo keeps the waits for each side of a junction separately, and shows the estimate for the side you came from.
